PROTOCOL STRATEGY

One master protocol or two separate trials?

Compare the evidence, timing and cost of studying monotherapy and a combination together or separately.

THE CHOICE

If a combination is ready later, should it join a shared-control trial or run in its own study?

WHY IT MATTERS

The choice affects control recruitment, time to an informative result and development cost.

MY CONTRIBUTION

Co-authored the framework with Naishu Kui and Biraj Guha, and co-mentored Naishu with Biraj. Naishu developed the R Shiny application.

WHAT THE EXAMPLE SHOWED

The model favored a master protocol mainly because of assumed operating costs; technical-success estimates were similar. The preference must be retested for each program.

Inspect the assumptions and limitsEvidence requirements, success definitions and model validation.

What needs checking before a program decision

  • Evidence first. Both selected designs use 75% power, the lowest value searched. Multiplicity rules differ. Agree clinical, statistical and component-evidence requirements before ranking feasible designs.
  • Success is model-defined. Technical success is crossing clinical hazard-ratio cutoffs, rather than rejecting the trial’s statistical null hypothesis. Joint success assumes independent overall- and progression-free-survival indicators.
  • The search is bounded. The grid contains 1,458 master and 81 distinct separate designs with fixed interim fractions. Sensitivity analysis found an earlier interim setting with higher value. The reported optimum is conditional on the search.
  • Economics are simplified. The model captures value erosion as standard of care changes; it does not apply a separate financial discount rate or a full cash-flow schedule. The outputs are modeled value, not realized savings.
  • Operations and regulatory assumptions matter. Recruitment is deterministic; separate trials do not reallocate unused capacity after one finishes enrollment. Both architectures use the same assumed component-related regulatory-success rule.
  • Validation remains necessary. Event timing, allocation, endpoint dependence and success-probability approximations need broader numerical assessment before use in another program.

Research manuscript: Master versus Separate Protocols in Oncology: A Quantitative Decision Framework — Naishu Kui, Abhishek Dubey and Biraj Guha. Internship sponsors and senior mentors: Arun Kumar and Burcin Simsek.

DOSE OPTIMIZATION

Choosing a dose worth taking forward

Bring efficacy, safety, tolerability and exposure together to identify a dose with a defensible benefit-risk profile for further development.

THE DEVELOPMENT PROBLEM

A higher dose may improve response while increasing serious adverse events or treatment interruptions. A single combined score can hide an unacceptable outcome.

PATIENT & DEVELOPMENT VALUE

Aim for meaningful benefit at a dose patients can tolerate. A clear benefit-risk assessment helps teams evaluate toxicity and treatment interruptions before committing patients and late-stage resources to a regimen.

MY CONTRIBUTION

Co-developed the two-stage benefit-risk framework and dose-selection rules with Shubhadeep Chakraborty, Arun Kumar and Ivan Chan. I connect the statistical decision criteria with clinical priorities and development strategy.

Used in real studies for dose selection

I apply this framework to dose-selection decisions in oncology studies, bringing an explicit assessment of efficacy, safety and tolerability into clinical team discussions.

Explore the dose-selection frameworkEndpoint acceptability, benefit-risk ranking and decision calibration.

THE DECISION FRAMEWORK

First establish acceptability. Then compare benefit–risk.

Inside the score: endpoints, probabilities and calibration

The illustration combines 6-month progression-free survival and objective response with five treatment-related safety and tolerability outcomes: death, discontinuation, dose reduction, dose delay and grade 3+ adverse events.

Each efficacy component measures the posterior probability of a clinically meaningful improvement over control. Each safety component measures the posterior probability of remaining below a prespecified excess-risk boundary.

Benefit–risk scoreGeometric mean of efficacy components
× geometric mean of safety components

The score is a ranking measure, not a joint probability of clinical success. Clinical thresholds still encode judgment. Simulations examine sample size, admissibility and selection under competing outcome profiles; confirmatory error control requires its own design plan.

Connect the method to the investment in evidence

A decision framework also needs enough information to distinguish doses. In a pivotal Phase 2/3 program, I helped the team agree to expand the initially proposed Phase 2 cohort so the dose decision would rest on a stronger evidence base.

The comparator averages raw endpoint differences. In three separate stress-test scenarios, ranking by the proposed benefit-risk score alone selected an inadmissible dose in 50.9–66.5% of simulations. This supports the need for the first-stage screen; it does not establish universal superiority across designs.

Clinical illustration: a consistent recommendation under six criteria sets

In a retrospective analysis of the CTLA-4 Probody study in melanoma (NCT03369223), two BMS-986249 plus nivolumab regimens were assessed against ipilimumab plus nivolumab. Arm F would have been selected under all six tested criteria sets.

Under moderate benefit and risk thresholds, both experimental arms were admissible; their benefit-risk scores were 0.11 for Arm C and 0.34 for Arm F. Tightening the criteria excluded Arm C while retaining Arm F.

This was a retrospective methodological illustration in a terminated development program, not a prospective selection or an approval outcome. It is distinct from the framework’s use in current studies.

Clinical-data illustration and modeling considerations

The illustrative approach uses patient bootstrapping and exposure–response models to estimate endpoint outcomes, then constructs probability-based benefit-risk components. Bootstrap replicates do not add independent patients, and the procedure does not automatically capture all model uncertainty.

Exposure–response associations can reflect disease severity, clearance and other prognostic factors. Model choice, confounding, extrapolation and the mapping from exposure to dose require clinical pharmacology review. This is an extension for development decisions, not a validated rule for individualized dosing.

PATIENT & DEVELOPMENT OPPORTUNITY

Explore whether existing tissue can answer useful biological questions before investing in additional assays. Prioritizing the right validation work could make better use of patient samples and research resources; clinical utility remains to be established.

MY CONTRIBUTION

Developed Multitask-TwoScale-HENet at NCI; coauthored the research manuscript with Peng Jiang. The framework combines patch-level learning, attention-based aggregation and patient-level prediction tasks.

THE RESEARCH FINDING

A shared model learned meaningful tissue patterns from patient-level labels and revealed exploratory immune associations. This work supports focused biomarker research; clinical utility requires further validation.

How the model learned—and what remains unproven

Method. An ImageNet-pretrained ResNet18 encodes 224 × 224 tissue patches into 512 features. Gated attention aggregates them into a patient representation. Joint tasks cover 93 genes, morphology, stage, molecular and immune subtypes, immune signatures and censored survival.

Evaluation. Evaluation used a patient-level 70:15:15 split, stain normalization and annotation-based analysis on BCSS. BCSS is derived from TCGA; independence from the training patients is not established here. The displayed maps are qualitative, not an independent clinical validation.

Limits. Associations involve training-related annotation families and are exploratory. No prospective biomarker utility or differential treatment benefit is established. Internal survival concordance was reported above 0.6, without a confidence interval or external survival validation.

EVIDENCE STRATEGY

Using existing evidence with a clear purpose

Use literature and real-world data to benchmark early signals and inform statistical considerations for registrational trials.

THE DEVELOPMENT PROBLEM

A single-arm signal is difficult to interpret without a relevant benchmark. Historical and real-world data offer additional context, but differences between populations, care and follow-up can distort comparisons.

PATIENT & DEVELOPMENT VALUE

Use existing evidence to focus new research on questions that matter for patients and investment. Relevant benchmarks can strengthen planning; population differences and uncertain comparisons must remain visible when deciding whether more prospective evidence is needed.

MY CONTRIBUTION

Develop model-augmented external-control and non-concurrent borrowing approaches. Use literature, real-world data, adjustment and causal methods to inform early benchmarking, component evaluation and statistical considerations for registrational trials.

HOW THE WORK IS USED

I translate external evidence into benchmarks and statistical considerations for trial planning, making the assumptions that drive sample size, timing and interpretation explicit.

Explore the approach to external evidenceBenchmarking, statistical considerations and sensitivity to assumptions.

The approach

01

Define the comparison

State the treatment question, target population and outcome before selecting external data.

02

Assess and adjust for differences

Examine population overlap, baseline prognostic factors, calendar time, endpoint definitions and follow-up. Choose adjustment or causal methods that match the question and available data.

03

Test the dependence on assumptions

Compare conclusions with and without borrowing. Examine sensitivity to unmeasured confounding, model choice, missingness and changing standards of care.

From external evidence to statistical considerations

Applied trial planning

I use literature and real-world data to translate early signals into plausible assumptions for registrational trial design. The analysis makes the effect of treatment setting, endpoint maturity and population differences explicit.

01

Anchor the benchmark to the target setting

Use trial-arm evidence and adjusted meta-regression to explore how line of therapy and geographic mix change expected response. Compare model forms and account for sparse, heterogeneous evidence.

02

Connect response and survival assumptions

Examine relationships between objective response, progression-free survival and overall survival. Use alternative models and regularization to build a range of planning scenarios.

03

Test what a combination could add

Vary the overlap in response to individual agents. Use real-world patterns to inform sensitivity ranges and assess how dependence assumptions change the combination benchmark.

How I use the results—and where inference stops

These analyses inform discussions of control outcomes, effect-size assumptions, event accrual, power and follow-up. I examine whether the design remains viable under less favorable assumptions before a development commitment.

Study-level endpoint relationships do not establish patient-level surrogacy or a causal treatment effect. Differences in case mix, subsequent therapy, follow-up and overlapping evidence can affect estimates. Real-world response patterns inform sensitivity analysis; they do not directly identify joint response to a new combination.

Planning assumptions and data used in a confirmatory treatment comparison have different roles. Formal borrowing or causal estimation needs a justified target population, comparability assessment and sensitivity analysis for unmeasured confounding.

THE DECISION OUTPUT

A range the trial can be designed around.

Translate external knowledge into defensible statistical considerations: an interpretable central scenario, plausible downside cases and a clear account of which assumptions drive sample size and timing.

COMBINATION DEVELOPMENT

What does each component contribute?

Build the evidence for a combination around its incremental clinical value.

THE DEVELOPMENT PROBLEM

A promising combination raises a second question: what does each component add, and can the available comparisons support that interpretation? Differences in patients, timing and treatment context can obscure the answer.

PATIENT & DEVELOPMENT VALUE

Ask whether adding a treatment contributes enough benefit to justify its additional toxicity and treatment burden. A clear component-evidence plan helps direct development resources toward combinations with a credible clinical rationale.

MY CONTRIBUTION

Apply external-data borrowing, adjustment and causal methods to contribution-of-components questions, connecting the analytical approach with the broader combination-development strategy.

THE CONTRIBUTION

I proposed combining a limited randomized component arm with external monotherapy evidence to support component evaluation and use development resources efficiently. The approach depends on a credible comparison and explicit assumptions.

Explore the component-evidence strategyComparisons, borrowing, adjustment and causal assumptions.

Right-size the randomized component arm

My proposed strategy keeps the component arm as small as the evidence objective permits and uses external monotherapy data to add information. The aim is to address what each component contributes while limiting additional enrollment and cost.

I connect that resource decision with population comparability, endpoint definitions, adjustment and causal assumptions. External information can strengthen an evidence package when those assumptions are credible; residual confounding and the need for randomized evidence remain part of the decision.

Make the component question explicit

01

Define the added value

Specify the combination-versus-component comparison, target population, endpoint and benefit-risk question. A combination-versus-control signal alone does not isolate each component’s contribution.

02

Choose an evidence structure

Consider what within-trial comparisons can establish and where historical, external or non-concurrent data add useful context. Connect the evidence plan to the master-versus-separate protocol choice.

03

Make causal assumptions visible

Use adjustment and causal methods to address observed population differences. Examine overlap, calendar-time changes and sensitivity to residual confounding before interpreting a component effect.

Experience and intended value

Applied methods · Evidence strategy

My work includes non-concurrent borrowing and model-augmented external evidence for component evaluation. The strategic objective is to identify whether the combination’s incremental benefit justifies its additional development, treatment and tolerability burden.

Interpretation & limits

Adjustment addresses measured differences under its assumptions; it does not establish exchangeability or eliminate unmeasured confounding. The appropriate evidence package depends on the specific component question. No numerical component-effect estimate or regulatory outcome is asserted here.
